# Study Protocol

**Radiation Safety Competency and Occupational Radiation Exposure among Cardiac Catheterization Laboratory Personnel**
*A Multicenter KAP Assessment and Prospective Educational Study*

---

## 1. Vision of the Study

Ionizing radiation is an essential component of many modern medical procedures, particularly in cardiac catheterization, interventional cardiology, electrophysiology, interventional radiology, and other radiation-based clinical specialties.

Healthcare professionals working in these environments may be repeatedly exposed to scattered radiation throughout their careers. However, radiation safety is not always treated as a formal clinical competency comparable to other essential competencies required before independent clinical practice.

This study addresses this gap by moving beyond the traditional assessment of radiation-safety knowledge and evaluating the broader concept of **Radiation Safety Competency**.

The study combines:

- Knowledge
- Attitude
- Practice
- Previous radiation-safety education
- Objective occupational radiation exposure using TLDs
- Assessment of workplace behavior
- A focused educational course
- Pre- and post-course assessment

The ultimate objective is to determine whether radiation-safety competency can be measured, whether important gaps exist, whether education can improve competency and workplace behavior, and whether these changes are associated with measurable reductions in occupational radiation exposure.

---

## 2. The Three Major Goals

### Goal 1 — Identify and Raise Awareness of a Potential Radiation-Safety Problem

Generate objective preliminary evidence regarding occupational radiation exposure and radiation-safety practices among healthcare professionals working in cardiac catheterization laboratories.

The study uses a relatively small, randomly selected prospective subgroup for objective TLD measurement. The purpose is **not** to claim that this sample represents all healthcare professionals or all medical radiation exposure in Saudi Arabia — it is intended to identify whether there is a measurable and potentially underrecognized radiation-safety problem that warrants broader investigation.

Findings may provide an important signal to healthcare authorities, hospital administrators, cardiac centers, radiation-safety officers, medical physicists, professional societies, and training and accreditation bodies regarding the need for stronger radiation-safety surveillance, education, monitoring, and protection.

The broader concern is the prevention of unnecessary or potentially excessive radiation exposure to patients, physicians and operators, nurses, radiographers, imaging personnel, technicians, and other healthcare workers.

> **Terminology note:** The study preferentially uses *occupational radiation exposure*, *patient radiation exposure*, or *potentially excessive radiation exposure* — not "radiation leakage," unless referring specifically to physical leakage from an imaging system or shielding barrier.

### Goal 2 — Develop a Radiation Safety Competency Index (RSCI)

Traditional studies ask *what does the practitioner know about radiation safety?* However, knowledge alone does not necessarily indicate safe clinical practice. A practitioner may have adequate theoretical knowledge but demonstrate poor workplace practices; another may have acquired appropriate habits through experience despite limited formal education.

This study therefore proposes the development and evaluation of a broader **Radiation Safety Competency Index (RSCI)** representing the practitioner's overall readiness to work safely in a radiation-based clinical environment:

```
Knowledge + Attitude + Practice + Training/Education → Radiation Safety Competency
```

Objective workplace observations may also contribute where feasible. The final structure, weighting, scoring system, and competency categories will be determined through statistical and psychometric validation rather than fixed in advance. See [`03-rsci-framework.md`](03-rsci-framework.md).

### Goal 3 — Support Competency-Based Radiation-Safety Certification

Determine whether the findings support a recommendation for standardized radiation-safety education and certification before independent practice in radiation-based clinical environments.

The proposed principle:

> **Radiation safety should be considered a clinical competency, not merely a body of knowledge.**

This could apply to professionals working in cardiac catheterization laboratories, interventional cardiology, electrophysiology, interventional radiology, and other radiation-based clinical specialties.

The study may ultimately support a model of mandatory standardized radiation-safety education with periodically renewed competency certification, analogous in principle to other competency-based clinical certifications such as BLS and PALS. The exact renewal interval is not predetermined by this study; it should be determined later according to evidence, professional standards, regulatory requirements, and national policy.

---

## 3. Central Research Question

> Are healthcare professionals working in radiation-based cardiac specialties adequately competent to practice safely with ionizing radiation, and can a focused radiation-safety educational course improve their knowledge, attitudes, and practices and translate these improvements into measurable reductions in occupational radiation exposure?

---

## 4. Study Design

Two complementary phases.

### Phase I — Multicenter KAP and Radiation Safety Competency Assessment

Assessment of radiation-safety knowledge, attitudes, practices, previous education, and potential competency among healthcare professionals working in cardiac catheterization laboratories.

- **Setting:** six cardiac centers in Riyadh
- **Instrument:** structured questionnaire covering four domains (see below and [`02-kap-questionnaire-draft.md`](02-kap-questionnaire-draft.md))

#### Domain 1 — Knowledge

Basic radiation physics; ionizing radiation; biological effects; occupational radiation risks; radiation exposure; sources of scattered radiation; relationship between distance and radiation exposure; time, distance, and shielding principles; radiation shielding; personal protective equipment; fluoroscopy-related exposure; radiation dose; radiation-dose reduction; safe positioning; radiation doses associated with commonly performed cardiac procedures.

Every knowledge question includes an **"I don't know"** option to distinguish genuine lack of knowledge from guessing.

#### Domain 2 — Attitude

Importance of radiation safety; personal risk from occupational radiation; risks to colleagues and patients; long-term consequences of occupational exposure; responsibility for radiation protection; importance of reducing unnecessary exposure; motivation to improve knowledge; willingness to participate in education; previous efforts to seek information; perceived adequacy of current institutional education; support for standardized education and competency-based certification.

Purpose: determine whether radiation safety is perceived as an essential component of clinical practice or merely a secondary technical issue.

#### Domain 3 — Practice

Self-reported practices: use of lead protection, thyroid protection, lead glasses, and available shielding; appropriate positioning; maintaining distance from the radiation source; awareness of scatter radiation; minimizing fluoroscopy time; dose awareness; efforts to reduce unnecessary exposure; appropriate use of protective equipment; behavior during routine procedures.

Where feasible, selected practices will also be evaluated **objectively** by the research team, because:

> Self-reported practice is not necessarily equivalent to observed practice.

#### Domain 4 — Previous Radiation-Safety Training

Previous education, formal courses, institutional training, timing and duration of most recent training, frequency of education, inclusion of radiation safety in professional training, perceived adequacy, interest in additional training.

#### Professional and Demographic Variables

Professional role; specialty; years of professional experience; years in radiation-based practice; frequency of participation in radiation-based procedures; type of cardiac procedures routinely performed or attended; previous radiation-safety training; institutional radiation-safety programs; availability of protective equipment.

### Phase II — Prospective Educational Study

A smaller group of healthcare professionals who routinely participate in cardiac catheterization procedures, selected using an appropriate sampling method to reduce selection bias, undergo objective occupational radiation measurement before and after a focused educational course.

| Step | Description |
|---|---|
| **Baseline** | KAP questionnaire; first TLD worn ~1 month during routine work; workload/procedure data collected where feasible (procedure count, type, complexity, fluoroscopy time, DAP/KAP, air kerma); selected practices objectively assessed where feasible |
| **Course** | Focused one-day radiation-safety educational course (see §6) |
| **Post-course** | Repeat KAP questionnaire; second TLD worn ~1 additional month; same procedural variables collected where feasible |

The comparison:

```
Baseline month:    TLD exposure + procedure workload + practice
        versus
Post-course month: TLD exposure + procedure workload + practice
```

This allows assessment of whether changes in radiation exposure remain present after accounting for differences in workload and procedural characteristics.

---

## 5. Blinding and Minimizing the Hawthorne Effect

Participants will **not** be informed of the specific pre–post comparison being evaluated — particularly that their occupational radiation exposure and workplace behavior before and after the course are being compared to determine whether the course reduces exposure. The objective is to allow participants to work as naturally as possible.

The study will nevertheless maintain appropriate informed-consent and research-ethics requirements. Any concealment of the specific research hypothesis or detailed outcome assessment will be implemented only to the extent permitted by the relevant research ethics committee.

For this reason the study should preferably be described as a:

> **Prospective educational study with partially blinded or undisclosed outcome assessment**

rather than automatically using the conventional term *double-blind study*.

---

## 6. Educational Course

A focused one-day radiation-safety course addressing:

- Basic radiation physics and biological effects of ionizing radiation
- Occupational radiation risks and sources of scattered radiation
- Time, distance, and shielding
- Radiation dose awareness and fluoroscopy-related exposure
- Appropriate positioning
- Personal protective equipment and radiation shielding
- Practical methods for reducing occupational exposure and safe workplace behavior
- Protection of operators, patients, nurses, radiographers, imaging personnel, technicians, and other healthcare workers

The course emphasizes **practical application in the cardiac catheterization laboratory**, rather than theoretical knowledge alone.

---

## 7. Hypothesis

A proportion of healthcare professionals working in cardiac catheterization laboratories may demonstrate gaps in radiation-safety competency, particularly among individuals who have not received formal or recent radiation-safety education.

The study does not assume that all participants have inadequate knowledge or practice; the purpose is to objectively measure the extent and distribution of any gaps.

### Primary Outcomes

| Phase | Outcome |
|---|---|

| **Phase II — educational** | Change in knowledge, attitude, and practice from before to after the course |
| **Phase II — occupational** | Change in TLD-measured occupational radiation exposure between baseline and post-course periods |

### Secondary Outcomes

Knowledge score; attitude score; practice score; training score; RSCI score; competency classification; change in objectively observed practice; number of procedures attended; procedure type; procedural complexity; fluoroscopy time; DAP/KAP; air kerma; professional role; years of experience; previous radiation-safety education; use of protective equipment; position relative to the patient and radiation source.

## 10. Why Competency Rather Than Knowledge?

The central conceptual argument:

> **Knowledge does not necessarily equal competency.**

Radiation safety in clinical practice requires the ability to translate knowledge into behavior. A practitioner may know that distance reduces exposure but still stand unnecessarily close to the patient; may know the importance of shielding but fail to use it consistently; may understand radiation risks but underestimate their personal occupational exposure.

Therefore:

- Knowledge → understanding
- **Competency → knowledge translated into safe clinical practice**

## 11. Regulatory and Standards Framework

The study's knowledge items, competency benchmarks, and educational course content are anchored to recognized international and national radiation-protection standards:

| Standard / body | Relevance to this study |
|---|---|
| **ICRP Publication 103** (2007 Recommendations) | Occupational effective dose limit: 20 mSv/year averaged over 5 years (max 50 mSv in any single year); system of justification, optimization (ALARA), and dose limitation |
| **ICRP Publication 118** | Revised eye-lens equivalent dose limit (20 mSv/year); tissue-reaction thresholds (cataract) |
| **ICRP Publication 120** | Radiological protection specifically in cardiology — directly applicable to cath lab practice |
| **OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1096 — Ionizing Radiation** | US occupational standard: quarterly whole-body dose limit of 1.25 rem (12.5 mSv); mandatory personal monitoring when a worker is likely to receive more than 25% of the quarterly limit; radiation-area caution signage; requirement to **instruct all personnel** in radiation-safety practices and the hazards involved — a regulatory precedent for the mandatory-education principle this study advances |
| **IAEA GSR Part 3** (International Basic Safety Standards) | The framework most national regulators (including Saudi Arabia) align with |
| **Saudi NRRC / SFDA regulations** | The governing national requirements for the participating centers; expert panel to confirm current dose limits and monitoring requirements |
| **NCRP reports** | Supporting guidance on occupational monitoring and fluoroscopy safety |

> **Note for the expert panel:** OSHA's numerical limits predate ICRP 103 and are less restrictive than current ICRP recommendations (12.5 mSv/quarter vs. 20 mSv/year). The questionnaire answer key and course content should teach the ICRP/IAEA/NRRC framework as the operative standard, while OSHA 1910.1096 is cited for its monitoring, signage, and — most importantly — its **mandatory personnel instruction** requirements, which directly support Goal 3 (competency-based certification).

---

## 12. Public Health and National Significance

The study seeks to determine whether there is evidence of an underrecognized occupational radiation-safety problem within medical practice, providing an early evidence base for:

- National radiation-safety surveillance
- Standardized education
- Occupational radiation monitoring
- Institutional radiation-safety programs
- Competency-based certification and periodic reassessment
- Development of national standards
- Further multicenter studies involving larger populations

The small prospective sample is **signal-generating and hypothesis-generating**, not a definitive national estimate of radiation exposure. If significant concerns are identified, the findings could justify a larger national study involving multiple medical specialties and healthcare institutions.

```
Before independent radiation-based clinical practice
          ↓
Standardized Radiation-Safety Course
          ↓
Competency Assessment
          ↓
Certification
          ↓
Independent Practice
          ↓

          ↓
```

The principle is similar to other competency-based clinical certifications (e.g., BLS, PALS), while recognizing that specific content, duration, and renewal interval must be established according to evidence and regulatory requirements.

## 15. Summary

```
ASSESS    → Multicenter KAP and competency assessment
MEASURE   → Objective occupational radiation exposure using TLD
EDUCATE   → Focused radiation-safety course
REASSESS  → KAP, practice, and TLD
VALIDATE  → Radiation Safety Competency Index
INFORM    → Healthcare authorities and institutions
RECOMMEND → Standardized, periodically renewed radiation-safety competency certification
```

> From radiation-safety awareness to measurable competency, from competency to safer practice, and from safer practice to reduced occupational radiation exposure.
